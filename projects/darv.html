Deployable Aerial Reconnaissance Vehicle (DAR-V)

Fixed-wing UAV for autonomous flight, aerial reconnaissance, and Avionics integration · SolidWorks design · FEA failure analysis.

What

Developed as a small team of engineering students, DAR-V is a fixed-wing UAV designed for autonomous flight. I led the avionics integration, overseeing the setup of the flight controller, GPS, and telemetry communication systems to ensure stable and coordinated operation. The design goal was a sustainable flight time of 20 minutes within a 3-mile operational radius, using a 3D-printed modular airframe optimized for lightweight performance and field serviceability.

How

The project began with selecting the airfoil, where the team chose the Eppler 193 through CFD analysis for its high lift-to-drag ratio and stable speed performance. From there, I helped model the airframe and internal layout in SolidWorks, with a central core designed to hold the system's avionics:

  • Flight controller: runs INAV, creates control loop processing for the aircraft
  • GPS: provides position hold and path routing
  • Video transmitter: sends the live camera feed to the PC for FPV and reconnaissance
  • Receiver: takes the controller signal and passes commands to the flight controller

On the avionics side, I configured the INAV flight controller to mesh with our PC application so we could see metrics in real time, and mapped the servo outputs and motor mixing for coordinated pitch, roll, and yaw control. Since the airframe was 3D printed, I also handled print orientation, wall thickness, and infill pattern to maximize strength and limit deformation under aerodynamic loading.

Results

After initial test flights, we recorded early flight times around the 3-minute mark before noticing mid-flight that the rear wings weren't responsive enough, making turns slow. Analyzing it, I traced the problem to the rear fuselage: its diameter was too narrow, and that single issue caused two failures at once.

First, the 3D-printed steering linkage was crammed into that tight space and was too weak to reliably drive the rear wings. Second, the servo bodies rubbed against the fuselage through their rotation, adding drag and delay in turns. Working through it with the team, I widened the fuselage diameter to open up clearance and replaced the cramped printed linkage with direct servo control to each wing, running through a roller bearing for smooth, precise actuation. Together these changes removed the rubbing and gave stronger, more direct control, noticeably improving flight stability, especially in turns.

A second round of flight testing reached flight times of about 9 minutes, but it also surfaced a separate, more serious failure: under repeated aerodynamic loading, the central fuselage fractured at the wing-root cross-section, where the wings' bending loads are reacted into the body. The wings themselves held, instead the printed body section gave way, snapping the airframe just behind the wing and separating the nose and avionics bay. Because this was the highest-bending-moment station on the airframe and a 3D-printed cross-section, it was a likely weak point, which is what drove my FEA study.

FEA Failure Analysis

With the failure location identified in flight, I ran a static structural analysis on the airframe in SolidWorks Simulation to confirm it, modeling a 3g flight-load case on the wings with the fuselage fixed. Applying a mesh control at the wing-root cross- section resolved the stress accurately at that connection. The analysis confirmed what the crashes had shown: stress concentrates where the wings meet the central body — the same station the airframe repeatedly failed in flight. Accounting for the epoxy-bonded steel rods and the anisotropy of the 3D-printed body at that joint explains why it became the fracture point.
